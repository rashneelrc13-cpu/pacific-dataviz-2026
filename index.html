The Rhythm That Guided Us

For generations, farmers in Fiji's Western Division read the rain like a clock. They knew its seasons, its pauses, and the patterns that helped them decide when to plant, prepare, and wait. But what happens when the clock begins to change? Using recorded rainfall data from the Western Division, this story explores how those patterns have shifted, and what that change means for the certainty farmers once relied on.

The wet season has actually gotten wetter

What this shows: the average amount of rain, in millimetres, recorded in each month at Nadi Airport, comparing two real periods: 1980 to 1999 and 2000 to 2024, drawn as a calendar wheel rather than a straight line. (Source: NOAA Global Historical Climatology Network Daily, station FJM00091680, built from Fiji Meteorological Service records. Real, complete daily data, no interpolation.) How to read it: each month sits at its own position around the circle, like hours on a clock, January at the top. The further a point sits from the centre, the more rain fell that month. The gold shape reaches further out than the teal shape in almost every direction, most clearly across the wet months from November to April. How this connects: this is the opposite of a simple drying story, the wet season has intensified, not faded. That is exactly the kind of shift that breaks a farming calendar built on "roughly this much rain, roughly this time of year": the season did not fade out predictably, it swung further in the other direction. Dry spells swing unpredictably, not steadily

What this shows: the longest unbroken run of dry days (under 1mm of rain) recorded in each decade at Nadi Airport, 1980 to today. (Source: NOAA Global Historical Climatology Network Daily, station FJM00091680, real daily data, no interpolation.) How to read it: each bar is one decade. Its height is the longest dry stretch, in days, that happened at some point during those ten years. Reading left to right, there is no steady climb: the worst stretch on record, 102 days, happened in the 1990s, not recently. How this connects: if the climate were simply drying out, this chart would climb steadily toward today. It does not. The pattern jumps up and down instead of trending in one direction, which is the same finding as the rest of this site stated a different way: not a one way slide, but a loss of the ability to predict which decade, or which year, will be the hard one. Hover any bar for the exact figure.

Where this affects real places

What this shows: four real places in Fiji's Western Division, the country's main farming and sugar growing region, marked on an illustrated map of the coastline. How to use it: hover a marked area for a quick fact about it. Click it to read, in plain terms, why that place depends on a predictable rain pattern. How this connects: everything on this page so far has been a number. This is where those numbers become a place. Each of these towns plans its planting and harvest around being able to predict the rain, not simply around how much falls in a year, which is exactly the thing the charts above show breaking down.

About this piece. The rhythm is built from two real, independent datasets, with no invented or interpolated figures anywhere. The main pulse and closing chart use Fiji's official annual rainfall anomaly record, 1979 to 2025, from the Pacific Community's Pacific Data Hub. The monthly rainfall comparison and the dry spell chart are computed directly from Nadi Airport's real daily rainfall record, 1980 to 2024 (NOAA Global Historical Climatology Network Daily, station FJM00091680, sourced from the Fiji Meteorological Service). Fiji's total rainfall shows no steady long term trend either wetter or drier, but it has become measurably more volatile, and its wet season has intensified rather than faded. The moving sky in the background is also driven by the real numbers: how bright, cloudy, and windy it looks shifts with the actual rainfall data for each period, not at random.
